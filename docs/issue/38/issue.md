## Body

`/squash-message` already says the title carries the `<essence>` of the change. On a `/sync-upstream` PR that word quietly resolves to the wrong thing: the most concrete-looking fact in front of the agent is the source SHA it just synced to, so the title comes out naming the transport rather than the cargo.

Concrete instance, from a repo that adopted this infrastructure — [vzakharov/vovazakharov.com#34](https://github.com/vzakharov/vovazakharov.com/pull/34). The proposal composed at PR-open was:

```
chore: sync the agent boilerplate forward to 4998149 (pr #34)
```

and what actually landed, after a retitle at merge:

```
chore: one job per loop skill, and a size cap on squash bodies (pr #34)
```

Same diff. The first tells a future reader nothing they can act on: `4998149` is a SHA in *another repository*, unresolvable from the log it sits in, and "sync forward" describes how the change arrived, not what it was. The second names what changed in the tree — which is what the skill says the record is for: "agents reconstructing how the codebase got this way, who read squash messages **first** and open diffs only when the log doesn't answer them." A sync-titled record guarantees the diff gets opened every time.

## The body has the mirror-image problem

Same PR, the body's opening paragraph:

> The boilerplate this repo vendors its agent infrastructure from kept editing it, and two commits had landed there since the last sync. Both apply here: every skill they touch is adopted, and neither is bound to the source's stack.

Four of the body's scarce ~50 lines spent on provenance — and provenance is already recorded, losslessly and precisely, one file over. `upstream.json`'s `lastSyncedSha` is committed as part of the very same change (Step 7). Worth noting that the per-commit `Ported from <repo>@<sha>` trailers Step 5 produces do **not** survive: a squash collapses them, so the watermark is the only durable machine-readable record, and it is a better one than prose.

The connection isn't worthless, though — "we vendor from a boilerplate that moved" is the honest answer to *why this change exists*, which is the opening paragraph's job. It just doesn't need a paragraph. One clause does it, leaving the rest of those lines for what changed.

## Suggested home

`sync-upstream` Step 8 is where the sync hands off to `/pr`, and it already says what belongs in the PR body (the triage table). It's the scoped place to say what belongs in the *squash record* — and being sync-specific, it leaves `/squash-message` (already long, already generic) untouched. Rough shape:

> **The squash record names the change, not the sync.** The title is what landed in *this* tree; a source SHA is unresolvable from this repo's log, and "sync forward to `<sha>`" describes the transport. The upstream connection is the honest *why*, so it earns one clause in the opening sentence and nothing more — `upstream.json`'s `lastSyncedSha`, committed in Step 7, is the precise record and doesn't need restating in prose.

One thing I'd argue **against**: generalising this into `/squash-message` as a broader "the transport is not the essence" rule. Sync is the only lane observed to fail this way, the evidence is a single PR, and that skill is the last file that needs another paragraph. If a dependency-bump or vendored-asset lane turns out to fail identically, that's the moment to lift the rule up a level.

## Adopter-facing angle

`ADOPTING.md` covers the watermark and the declined-reasons discipline at length, but says nothing about how a sync should read in the log afterwards — and adopters are exactly the audience that inherits this failure mode, since every one of their syncs is a `chore:` PR whose subject is the first thing their `git log` shows. If Step 8 grows the rule, a pointer from `ADOPTING.md`'s sync section is probably the right amount, rather than a second copy of it.
